Campus Cup Tournament Ops

Early 2025 – March 2026 · Chandigarh University LAN Finals

Objective

In early 2025, five of us formed a team specifically to compete in the JioGames Valorant Campus Cup, India's national collegiate Valorant tournament. The goal from day one wasn't just to compete; it was to win it outright. Over the following year we built toward that through college qualifiers, national qualifiers, and finally the LAN finals at Chandigarh University in March 2026, where we won ahead of a field of over 500 competing teams nationwide.

Team & Roles

I served as In-Game Leader (IGL), Team Lead, and Strategist, a combined role with two distinct kinds of responsibility. As IGL, in-match decisions were mine alone: shot-calling, real-time adjustments, and correcting mistakes as they happened, since a five-person team can't caucus mid-round. Off the server, bigger decisions, like which agent compositions to run, and how much and when to practice, were made collectively as a team, with me facilitating rather than dictating.

That split mattered: fast, unilateral calls under pressure, but shared ownership of the direction we were building toward.

Timeline

  • Team formation (early 2025): assembled the roster specifically for this tournament cycle
  • College qualifiers: the proving ground to advance beyond our own institution
  • National qualifiers: competing against qualifying teams from colleges across the country
  • LAN Finals (March 2026): Chandigarh University, the final in-person event where we won

Tools & Process

Coordination ran through two channels: Discord for day-to-day communication and scheduling, and Valoplant for structured, team-based strategy study. Rather than over-scheduling, we deliberately kept practice sessions less frequent but fully attended. Every scrim happened with the whole team present, which we found more valuable than a packed but inconsistent schedule.

Weekly review was built around VOD analysis: watching our own matches to identify recurring mistakes, and separately studying opponents' VODs ahead of matches to prepare targeted strategies against their known tendencies.

Risks & Resolutions

  • Player friction. Normal in any high-pressure team environment. As team lead, I treated this as an ongoing communication responsibility rather than a one-time fix, addressing tension directly and early, before it could affect practice or matches.
  • Strategy plateaus. Periods where our approach against certain team compositions stopped working. Drawing on IGL experience, I could usually diagnose and adjust strategy quickly rather than letting a losing pattern continue across matches.

KPIs Tracked

The primary metric we tracked was round-loss reasons, categorizing why we lost each round (execution error vs. strategic mismatch vs. individual mistake) rather than just tracking win/loss at the map level.

That granularity is what let us catch fixable problems fast: several matches turned from losing to winning within the same session once we identified that a round was being lost to a small, repeatable mistake rather than a genuine skill gap.

Outcome

Winner, National JioGames Valorant Campus Cup 2026, Chandigarh University LAN Finals, March 2026, ahead of a field of 500+ competing teams nationwide. The win drew online news coverage of the team.

Retrospective

The clearest lesson from this year: consistency beats volume. We practiced less often than many competing teams, but never partially. Every session had the full roster, which kept both skill and trust building together rather than one outpacing the other.

The other habit worth carrying forward: separating diagnosis from fix. Tracking round-loss reasons specifically, rather than just results, meant strategy adjustments were targeted rather than guesswork, a habit that maps directly onto how I approach tracking blockers on any project today.
